Shielding & EMI basics

Section 1.0 // Learning Objectives

Module Objectives

  • Define EMI and trace its common sources inside an aircraft.
  • Demonstrate the three primary layout techniques used to eliminate EMI coupling.
  • Explain why a shielded cable's drain wire must only be grounded at one end.
Section 2.0 // Introduction

Why Does This Matter?

A newly installed digital audio panel operates flawlessly on battery power. However, the exact moment the pilot turns on the aircraft alternator, a loud, piercing whine that changes pitch precisely with engine RPM floods the pilot's headset. The technician inspects the wiring: the sensitive mic audio signal wire was zip-tied directly alongside the high-current alternator output wire, and to make matters worse, the audio cable's outer shield was left entirely disconnected (floating). The system suffered massive Electromagnetic Interference (EMI). This lesson teaches you how to design harnesses that are completely immune to this invisible threat.

Section 3.0 // Core Principles

What You Need To Know

EMI (Electromagnetic Interference)

EMI is destructive, unwanted electrical energy that magnetically couples from a 'noisy' emitting circuit into an adjacent 'quiet' receiving circuit, vastly degrading analog signal quality and corrupting digital databuses. Common high-emission EMI sources include:

  • Alternator output lines (AC ripple noise)
  • Strobe light power supplies (high-voltage capacitive switching spikes)
  • Ignition harnesses (massive high-voltage pulses)
  • Transmitting radio coaxial cables (RF radiation)

Three Mandatory EMI Reduction Techniques

  1. 1. Physical Separation: The most effective defense. Route sensitive signal wires and noisy power wires in completely distinct bundles, maintaining an absolute minimum of 3 inches of physical separation throughout the airframe.
  2. 2. Twisted-Pair Wiring: Signals (like databus lines) are transmitted down two identical conductors twisted tightly together (e.g., 6 twists per foot). Any magnetic noise wave passing through the bundle induces an equal but completely opposite voltage in each overlapping twist, perfectly canceling the noise out to zero.
  3. 3. Shielded Cables: A dense, conductive metal braid woven entirely around the internal signal wires. It physically intercepts and captures incoming electromagnetic fields before they can reach the sensitive copper core.

Shield Termination (The Single-Point Ground Rule)

The absolute most critical rule of avionics shielding: Ground the braided shield drain wire at ONE end only (typically at the receiver or the avionics chassis end).

Why? Grounding a shield at *both* ends physically connects two different airframe locations. Due to airframe resistance, those two points often sit at fractionally different voltage potentials. This difference drives a massive current straight through the shield braid from one end to the other. This phenomenon is called a Ground Loop. A ground loop physically transforms your protective shield into a massive, noise-radiating antenna — creating the exact interference you were paid to eliminate.

Floating Shields Provide Zero Protection

A shielded cable where the drain wire is not connected to ground at either end provides absolute zero EMI protection. The metallic shield intercepts the magnetic waves, but without a ground path to dump that energy into the airframe, the energy simply bleeds straight through the insulation and directly onto the signal conductor.

Section 4.0 // Practical Application

On The Job

Standard Operating Procedure

When terminating shielded cables, meticulously unbraid the shield, form the drain wire pigtail, and apply a solder sleeve to extend the ground lead. Verify on the schematic which end gets grounded. If you ground both, you will spend three days chasing a mystery audio whine. If you ground neither, the shield is dead weight. Finally, physically separate the audio, databus, and power bundles as early as possible behind the instrument panel.

Reference Terms

EMI Reduction Techniques

EMI Reduction Techniques

To reduce electromagnetic interference in aircraft wiring: separate power wires from signal wires (minimum 3-inch separation for most installations), use twisted-pair cables for differential signals, and use shielded cables for sensitive analog or digital signals near high-current wiring.

Shield Termination — One End Only

Tap to expand definition
Shield Termination — One End Only

The standard practice for cable shield termination is to ground the shield drain wire at one end only (typically the equipment/receiver end), leaving the other end floating — this prevents ground loops where differences in ground potential between two points create circulating current that radiates EMI.

Floating Shield — No EMI Protection

Tap to expand definition
Floating Shield — No EMI Protection

A shielded cable provides EMI protection only when the shield is connected to ground, creating a low-impedance path for induced interference currents to flow to ground rather than onto the signal wire. A floating shield (ungrounded at both ends) cannot divert induced currents and provides no practical shielding.

Shielded Wire — EMI Function

Tap to expand definition
Shielded Wire — EMI Function

Shielded wire provides two-way EMI protection: (1) incoming — the conductive shield prevents external electromagnetic fields from coupling onto the signal conductor; (2) outgoing — the shield contains signal emissions and prevents them from interfering with adjacent systems.

EMI (Electromagnetic Interference)

EMI (Electromagnetic Interference)

Unwanted external electrical energy magnetically coupling into a circuit and heavily degrading or corrupting its signal.

Ground Loop

Ground Loop

A destructive conductive loop formed by mistakenly grounding a cable shield at both ends, allowing circulating currents that radiate immense interference.

Drain Wire

Drain Wire

The uninsulated pigtail conductor formed from the shielding braid, specifically used to terminate the shield to an airframe ground point.

Twisted-Pair

Two signal conductors uniformly twisted together specifically to magnetically cancel induced external noise.
