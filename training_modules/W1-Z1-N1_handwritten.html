Hazard Recognition & the Stop-Work Mindset

Learn to identify unsafe conditions, exercise your stop-work authority, and understand why near-miss reporting is the most powerful safety tool in your shop.

Section 1

Why Does This Matter?

You are pulling wire behind an avionics rack when you notice the floor is wet from a leaking roof drain. Nobody has slipped yet. Do you keep working, or do you stop?

In aviation maintenance, your ability to recognize a hazard and stop work before someone gets hurt is the single most important safety skill you will develop. The Part 145 shop floor is not a place where you "hope for the best."

The Real-World Impact

Industry data shows that for every serious accident, there are approximately 300 unreported near-misses that preceded it. Each near-miss is a free warning — ignore it, and the next event may not be a miss.

Section 2

What You Need to Know

A hazard is any condition, practice, or situation that has the potential to cause injury, damage, or loss — even if nothing bad has happened yet. A wet floor is a hazard whether or not someone has slipped on it.

Stop-Work Authority

In a Part 145 repair station, every technician has both the right and the obligation to stop work when they identify an unsafe condition. You do not need a supervisor's permission. The correct sequence is:

  1. 1
    Stop — Cease the task immediately. Do not try to "finish this one thing first."
  2. 2
    Secure — Make the area or equipment safe. De-energize circuits, barricade wet floors, isolate the hazard.
  3. 3
    Report — Notify your supervisor and document what you observed. This is not optional.

The Near-Miss

A near-miss is an unplanned event that did NOT result in injury or damage, but had the potential to do so. The technician who slips on a wet floor and catches himself on a bench — that is a near-miss.

Why Near-Misses Must Be Reported

Near-misses must be reported and investigated. They are not "no harm, no foul" events. Reporting a near-miss is how your shop fixes the hazard before someone gets hurt. A near-miss is a warning you only get once.

On the Job

Applying This in Your Shop

Your Action Rule

When you see something that does not look right — a wet floor, a frayed cord, an unsecured panel — stop what you are doing, make it safe, and report it. Do not assume someone else will handle it. Near-misses are free lessons. Report them every time.
