Maintenance Regulations & Documentation

Master the regulatory framework that governs every FAA-certificated repair station. This module covers 14 CFR Parts 43 and 145, required documentation, maintenance classifications, and the compliance standards you need for CAET certification.

25 min 6 sections 31 objectives

1. The Regulatory Framework

Every piece of maintenance performed on a certificated aircraft in the United States is governed by the Federal Aviation Regulations (FARs). As an avionics technician working in a repair station, you need to understand which regulations apply to you, which apply to the shop, and which apply to the work itself.

14 CFR Part 145 — The Repair Station

Part 145 The FAA regulation that establishes performance rules and certification requirements for repair stations performing maintenance on aircraft and aircraft components.

Part 145 governs the repair station as an organization. It covers the station's certification, required personnel, facilities, equipment, quality control systems, and operational standards. If you are working in an FAA-certificated repair station, Part 145 is the regulation that defines your shop's authority and obligations.

ACE Says
Think of Part 145 like a restaurant health code — it sets the mandatory operating standards a shop must meet to stay certified. Without it, you cannot legally open for business.

A Part 145 station may only perform work within its specific ratings and classes listed in its operations specifications. Work outside those ratings is not authorized, regardless of how qualified individual technicians may be.

ACE Says
A licensed electrician cannot perform plumbing work just because they are in the same building. Same principle — your station's ratings define your scope.

14 CFR Part 43 — The Work Itself

Part 43 The FAA regulation governing performance rules for aircraft maintenance, preventive maintenance, rebuilding, and alterations — applying to any authorized person performing this work.

While Part 145 governs the station, Part 43 governs the work. It applies to everyone touching the aircraft — whether they work in a repair station, airline, or even an owner-operator performing preventive maintenance.

Key Distinction

Part 145 = rules for the shop (organization)

Part 43 = rules for the work (maintenance performance)

FAA Definition of Maintenance

Maintenance Per FAA regulations: inspection, overhaul, repair, preservation, and replacement of parts — explicitly excluding preventive maintenance, which is a separate regulatory category.
ACE Says
Maintenance is like a full car service package covering inspections and repairs. Preventive maintenance is the DIY oil change the owner does themselves — separate category, separate rules.

Preventive maintenance under Part 43 is limited to certificated pilots on aircraft they own or operate, and only for simple tasks explicitly listed in Part 43 Appendix A.

Quick Check
Which regulation governs the performance rules for the repair station as an organization?
A 14 CFR Part 43
B 14 CFR Part 91
C 14 CFR Part 145
D 14 CFR Part 65
Part 145 governs the repair station organization. Part 43 governs the maintenance work itself. Part 91 covers aircraft operating rules. Part 65 covers airmen certification.

2. Documentation & Record-Keeping

Documentation is the backbone of aviation maintenance. Every piece of work must be recorded completely and accurately. Incomplete records can ground an aircraft, void warranties, and create serious legal liability.

Maintenance Records — 14 CFR §43.9

§43.9 Record Per 14 CFR §43.9, a maintenance record must include: description of work performed, date completed, name of person performing work, certificate number, and a return-to-service signature.

These five elements are non-negotiable. Every maintenance entry must contain all of them:

  1. Description of work performed (and parts used)
  2. Date completed
  3. Name of person performing the work
  4. Certificate number of the technician
  5. Return-to-service signature/statement
ACE Says
Think of it like a doctor's note after surgery — it must say what was done, when, by whom with their license number, and that the patient is cleared to go.
Scenario

A tech completes an avionics installation and writes: "Installed GPS unit per STC. Aircraft N12345. Signed, J. Smith."

What's missing? The date, the certificate number, and the return-to-service statement. Three of five required elements are absent. The aircraft N-number, while useful, is not a §43.9 requirement.

FAA Form 8130-3 — The Airworthiness Approval Tag

Form 8130-3 An Airworthiness Approval Tag used to certify that an aircraft part or component meets airworthiness standards and to trace its origin and maintenance history.

When a part arrives at the shop with an 8130-3 attached, it tells you two critical things: the part has been certified as airworthy and its origin is traceable. This is essential for parts traceability on certificated aircraft.

ACE Says
Think of an 8130-3 as a birth certificate and health record combined — it proves where the part came from and that it has been properly vetted.

Technical Data Requirements

Being on the capability list authorizes the type of work, but Part 145 also requires the current revision of the manufacturer's maintenance manual before work begins. Both authorization and current data are required.

Important

If the current revision of the manufacturer's maintenance manual is not available in the shop, maintenance cannot legally proceed — even if the work is on your capability list.

The Quality Control department and accountable manager are responsible for ensuring current technical data is available and used during maintenance. This is a systemic responsibility, not just an individual practitioner's burden.

Quick Check
Which of these is a required element of a maintenance record per 14 CFR §43.9?
A Aircraft's flight hours
B Aircraft registration number
C Work order reference number
D Technician's certificate number
The five required elements are: description of work, date, name, certificate number, and return-to-service statement. Flight hours, registration number, and work order number are not §43.9 requirements.

3. Maintenance Classifications

The FAA distinguishes between major and minor alterations and repairs. This classification determines the documentation requirements, who can approve the work, and what data is needed.

Major Alteration (Part 43 Appendix A)

Major Alteration A change to type design that might appreciably affect structural strength, flight characteristics, or other airworthiness qualities — and is not listed in the aircraft specifications or type certificate data sheet.
ACE Says
A major alteration is like a structural renovation that changes a building's load-bearing capacity — it needs engineering review and a permit. Replacing a light fixture? That's minor.

STC Installations

STC A Supplemental Type Certificate — an FAA approval that permits modification of a type-certificated aircraft in a manner not covered by the original type certificate.

An STC installation that includes a flight manual supplement is classified as a major alteration, regardless of whether the part itself holds a TSO. Why? Because it changes the aircraft's approved operating limitations or procedures.

ACE Says
A TSO on the part is like a UL listing on an appliance — the part is approved. But modifying your home's electrical panel to install it still requires a permit.

TSO-for-TSO Replacement

Replacing a TSO part with another TSO part of the same type, done per approved data, is generally not a major alteration. The type design is not changed in a way that appreciably affects airworthiness.

Quick Check
An STC installation includes a flight manual supplement. How is this classified?
A Minor alteration — if the part has a TSO
B Major alteration — regardless of TSO status
C Preventive maintenance
D It depends on the aircraft type
An STC installation with a flight manual supplement is always a major alteration because it changes the aircraft's approved operating limitations. The TSO status of the part is irrelevant to this classification.

4. Inspections & Testing Requirements

Certain avionics systems require periodic testing by authorized facilities. Understanding which regulations govern these inspections — and what documentation is required — is essential.

Pitot-Static & Transponder Inspections

91.411 Governs altimeter and pitot-static system testing. Appropriately rated repair stations are authorized to perform these inspections.
91.413 Governs transponder testing and inspection. Required every 24 calendar months for aircraft operating in transponder-required airspace.
ACE Says
Like a specialty clinic authorized for specific procedures — the facility's rating matters, not just the individual practitioner's credentials. And like a mandatory vehicle safety inspection, it's on a 24-month calendar cycle regardless of usage.

Test Documentation

Per Part 43, altimeter and transponder test records must include:

  • Test date
  • Equipment used (with calibration status)
  • Test results
  • Return-to-service statement

Test Equipment Calibration

Before use in a Part 145 shop, specialized test equipment must have a current calibration label showing it is within its calibration interval. Out-of-calibration equipment produces invalid results and non-compliant records.

ACE Says
Verify calibration like verifying a thermometer against a known reference — if the tool is inaccurate, your measurements are meaningless.
Quick Check
How often does 14 CFR 91.413 require transponder testing?
A Every 12 calendar months
B Every 100 flight hours
C Every 24 calendar months
D Only after a maintenance event
14 CFR 91.413 requires transponder testing every 24 calendar months. It's a calendar-based requirement, not flight-hour-based, and applies regardless of how much the aircraft has been flown.

5. Shop Practices & Standards

Beyond the regulatory requirements, a Part 145 avionics shop must follow specific standards and best practices to maintain safety, quality, and compliance.

Wiring Standards

AC 43.13-1B Advisory Circular covering acceptable methods, techniques, and practices for aircraft inspection and repair. Chapter 11 is the primary wiring standard for avionics installation.

AC 43.13-1B (or the aircraft manufacturer's wiring data when it exists) is the primary standard for avionics wiring installation. When manufacturer-specific data exists, it takes precedence.

Connector Pin Work

Terminated wires must be inserted into or removed from circular connectors using the proper manufacturer-specified insertion and extraction tools. Improvised tools damage pins and create intermittent faults that are extremely difficult to troubleshoot.

ACE Says
Like removing a SIM card with the proper eject tool — the right tool protects the socket from damage that would be difficult to see and even harder to troubleshoot.

Troubleshooting Methodology

In a Part 145 shop, the first step when troubleshooting any fault is to consult the manufacturer's maintenance manual. Approved data must guide the diagnostic procedure before any components are replaced.

Scenario

An intermittent NAV flag appears on a VOR receiver. A new tech suggests swapping the receiver immediately.

Correct approach: Consult the manufacturer's maintenance manual first. The manual's troubleshooting tree may identify a wiring issue or antenna problem without replacing any LRUs.

FOD & Workplace Safety

Keeping walkways and work areas free of clutter prevents FOD (Foreign Object Damage/Debris), trip hazards, and tool loss — all of which directly affect airworthiness and safety.

ACE Says
Think of it like a hospital operating room floor — a dropped screw creates both a personnel hazard and a risk of ending up somewhere it should never be.
Quick Check
What is the first step when troubleshooting a fault in a Part 145 shop?
A Consult the manufacturer's maintenance manual
B Replace the suspect LRU
C Run a self-test on the unit
D Check the circuit breaker panel
Approved data must guide the diagnostic procedure. Always start with the manufacturer's maintenance manual — it defines the approved troubleshooting methodology and may reveal the root cause without replacing any components.

6. Safety, Compliance & Personnel

A Part 145 shop's compliance obligations extend beyond the aircraft to include personnel requirements, workplace safety, and hazardous material management.

Pre-Employment Drug Testing

A technician must pass a pre-employment drug test before performing safety-sensitive functions if the repair station's FAA-approved drug testing program requires it. No clearance, no work on aircraft.

Recurrent Training

Most Part 145 repair station manuals require recurrent training for maintenance personnel at least every 24 months. This ensures skills and knowledge stay current with evolving standards and procedures.

Hazardous Materials — Safety Data Sheets

SDS Safety Data Sheet — the document required to be readily available for each hazardous material used in the shop, covering hazards, PPE requirements, first aid, and disposal procedures.

Fire Safety

Class C Fire A fire involving energized electrical equipment. Non-conductive agents (CO₂, dry chemical) must be used — never water, which conducts electricity.

Hearing Protection

Hearing protection is critical in an avionics shop because noise-induced hearing loss is cumulative and permanent. Each unprotected exposure adds to total damage, even when individual exposures seem tolerable.

ACE Says
Think of hearing protection like sunscreen for your ears — you don't feel the damage happening, but it accumulates with every unprotected exposure.

Quick Diagnostic Skills

Stuck Microphone Diagnosis

A constant tone on a comm frequency instead of ATC voice responses indicates a stuck PTT (Push-To-Talk) — a microphone transmitting continuously, blocking the frequency for all users. It's like someone holding down a walkie-talkie transmit button.

Parasitic Draw Diagnosis

To confirm a faulty switch as the source of a parasitic draw, place an ammeter in series with the circuit and activate the suspect switch while monitoring current. A current increase confirms the switch is the source. It's like checking which faucet is draining your water pressure — turn them on one at a time while watching the flow meter.

Mono Audio in GA Aircraft

Mono (single-channel) audio is typically used in general aviation aircraft for intercom communications and speaker monitoring — a single audio channel routed to all headsets and speakers. Unlike stereo, there is no distinct left/right separation.

Quick Check
What document must be readily available for each hazardous material in a Part 145 shop?
A Material Safety Compliance Certificate
B Safety Data Sheet (SDS)
C EPA Disposal Authorization Form
D FAA Form 8130-3
The Safety Data Sheet (SDS) is required for every hazardous material. It covers hazards, PPE requirements, first aid procedures, and disposal requirements. It must be readily accessible to anyone working with the material.
